Theory

This session prepares the skills used in every experiment of the course: connecting meters, reading them, estimating the uncertainty and drawing a straight-line graph.

1. Meters and how to connect them

• Ammeter (current, A or mA): in series; very small resistance. • Voltmeter (potential difference, V): in parallel across the component; very large resistance. • Ohmmeter (resistance, Ω): only on a component disconnected from the supply. • A digital multimeter (AVO-meter) does all three; choose DC (=) or AC (~) and the range.

2. Choosing the range

Start on the highest range, then move down until the reading is as large as possible without “OL / 1.” (over-range). A smaller range gives more significant figures.

3. Reading an analogue meter

Find the value of the smallest division: (range) ÷ (number of divisions). Read with your eye directly above the needle (no parallax). The reading uncertainty is about half a division.

4. Uncertainty and percentage error

relative uncertainty = Δx/x,   percentage error = |experimental − accepted| / accepted × 100 %

For a product or quotient, add the relative uncertainties: if R = V/I, then ΔR/R = ΔV/V + ΔI/I. Keep the number of significant figures of the least precise measurement.

5. The straight-line graph

Write the law in the form y = m x + c. Choose a scale that uses most of the graph paper, plot the points with small crosses, draw the best straight line (points scattered evenly on both sides — do not join the dots), and find the slope from two points on the line that are far apart (not data points):

slope m = (y2 − y1) / (x2 − x1)  (with units)

In this course: V–I gives R or Z; ln(V0/V)–t gives 1/τ; Vpp–Vrms gives 2√2; I–tan θ gives K; V2–V1 gives N2/N1.
