Experiment 6: Voltage Transformation with a Transformer

Measuring the secondary voltage V2 against the primary voltage V1 for different turns ratios

⚠️ Safety: use only the low-voltage AC supply (never the mains directly); raise V₁ gradually and never above 10 V; never connect a DC supply to the primary (the current becomes very large and the coil overheats); a step-up arrangement gives a higher output voltage — do not touch the secondary terminals.

Theory

A transformer is two coils wound on the same soft-iron core. Linked to Chapter 5 – Faraday’s law of induction and Lenz’s law.

1. How it works

The alternating current in the primary coil (N1 turns) produces an alternating magnetic flux ΦB in the core. The core guides almost all of this flux through the secondary coil (N2 turns). The changing flux induces an emf in every turn (Faraday’s law):

V1 = N1 dΦB/dt,   V2 = N2 dΦB/dt  →  V2 / V1 = N2 / N1

• N2 > N1: step-up transformer. • N2 < N1: step-down transformer. • The frequency is not changed. • A transformer does not work on steady DC: a constant current gives a constant flux, so no emf is induced.

2. The graph

For each turns ratio, plot V2 (y-axis) against V1 (x-axis). Each gives a straight line through the origin whose slope is the measured voltage ratio. Compare the slope with N2/N1:

voltage-ratio coupling = slope ÷ (N2/N1) × 100 %

This is the measured voltage ratio as a percentage of the ideal turns ratio — it is not the power efficiency. With the secondary open it can approach 100 % while no power is delivered. The true efficiency is η = Pout/Pin, measured with a load (taking the power factor into account).

A real (student) transformer gives a slightly smaller value than the ideal one because some flux leaks out of the core and the coils have resistance.

Apparatus: demountable transformer (U-core and yoke, coils of 500 and 1000 turns), low-voltage variable AC supply, two AC voltmeters, connecting wires.
